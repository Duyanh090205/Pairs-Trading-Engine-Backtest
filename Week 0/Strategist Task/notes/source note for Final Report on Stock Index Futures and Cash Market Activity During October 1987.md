# Source note for *Final Report on Stock Index Futures and Cash Market Activity During October 1987*

- Portfolio insurance was a specialized form of short hedging, also called **"dynamic asset allocation."** Its goal was to limit how much a stock portfolio lost in a falling market while still letting the investor keep gains in a rising market (CFTC 1988, pp. 25-26).
- The strategy worked by changing the portfolio’s target **equity exposure** as the market moved. The report says the model called for specified changes in exposure as the market rose or fell (CFTC 1988, pp. 25-26).
- In practice, managers usually used **stock index futures** to make those adjustments instead of immediately selling stocks or buying put options, mainly because futures were cheaper and more efficient (CFTC 1988, p. 26).
- The basic logic of dynamic hedging was mechanical: when prices fell, the model called for **less equity exposure**, so managers sold futures; when prices rose, the model called for **more equity exposure**, so they could buy futures back or add exposure (CFTC 1988, pp. 25-26).
- The strategy sells when markets fall because it is trying to **cut downside risk**. The report explains that short hedging is used to limit downside exposure, and portfolio insurance was a more specialized version of that same idea (CFTC 1988, p. 25).
- CFTC data show this selling rose sharply during the crash period. Gross futures selling by institutional hedgers increased from about **3,500 contracts on October 14** to **14,600 on October 16**, **32,700 on October 19**, and **33,600 on October 20** (CFTC 1988, p. 49).
- This becomes dangerous when many institutions do it at the same time because it creates a large wave of **same-direction selling** in the futures market. The report says portfolio hedge sales were especially heavy on October 16, 19, and 20, which helped create large futures discounts when buying support was weak (CFTC 1988, Summary pp. vi-viii; p. 49).
- Still, the CFTC’s main conclusion is careful: it does **not** say portfolio insurance alone caused the crash. It says the evidence does not support the idea that index arbitrage and portfolio hedging mechanically interacted to cause the huge drop on October 19; instead, the broader selloff appears to have reflected a major change in investor perceptions (CFTC 1988, Summary pp. iv-v, vii-x; p. 45).
- Another important point for a memo: many firms did **not** follow their models fully in the middle of the turmoil. The report says some funds reduced or abandoned the selling implied by their plans, and some managers said they would have sold stocks anyway if futures had not been available (CFTC 1988, Summary pp. ix-x; p. 51).
- The lesson regulators and analysts took from the crash was mainly about **market structure and oversight**, not just blaming one strategy. The report calls for better data collection on trading strategies, stronger interagency and interexchange coordination, and careful attention to price limits because rules in one market can shift pressure into another market (CFTC 1988, pp. 182-188).

## Source citation

Commodity Futures Trading Commission, Division of Economic Analysis, & Division of Trading and Markets. (1988, January). *Final Report on Stock Index Futures and Cash Market Activity During October 1987*. Washington, DC: U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission.
