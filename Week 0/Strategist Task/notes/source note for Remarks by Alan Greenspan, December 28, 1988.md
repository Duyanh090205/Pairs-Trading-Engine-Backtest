# Source Note for *Remarks by Alan Greenspan, December 28, 1988*

- In this speech, Greenspan treats **portfolio insurance** as a strategy that let investors stay heavily invested in stocks while believing they could quickly cut exposure and limit losses if prices started falling (Greenspan, 1988, p. 7).
- He links portfolio insurance especially to **aggressive, model-driven, computer-executed programs**, not just discretionary selling by individual investors (Greenspan, 1988, p. 7).
- **Dynamic hedging** works by changing positions as prices move. In Greenspan’s description, these programs “key off” market-price movements and respond quickly as conditions change (Greenspan, 1988, p. 8).
- In practice, that means when prices fall, the strategy tries to **shed risk by reducing long equity exposure**, often through fast trades tied to derivatives markets (Greenspan, 1988, pp. 7–8).
- The strategy sells when markets fall because its whole purpose is to **limit losses in a declining market**; once prices turn down, the model tells investors to cut exposure (Greenspan, 1988, p. 7).
- Greenspan says this became a problem because these programs did not just react to falling prices—they **reinforced** the selling pressure already in the market (Greenspan, 1988, p. 8).
- It became dangerous when many institutions did it at the same time because, even if **one investor** might reduce risk successfully, **the system as a whole cannot** all get out at once (Greenspan, 1988, p. 8).
- Their near-simultaneous efforts to contain losses **pushed the system beyond its limits**, worsened execution problems, and created losses bigger than investors expected when they adopted the strategy (Greenspan, 1988, p. 8).
- Greenspan also argues that modern technology and the size of institutional trading made this worse: huge volumes of sell orders could hit the market in **seconds or minutes**, making the drop much more sudden (Greenspan, 1988, pp. 8–9).
- Once prices were falling sharply, a **feedback loop** formed: falling prices raised uncertainty, buyers stepped back, execution capacity weakened, and more investors rushed to sell while they still could (Greenspan, 1988, pp. 9–10, 14).
- One lesson analysts and regulators took from the crash was that faith in **dynamic hedging as reliable protection** had weakened; Greenspan says disappointment with its performance reportedly led to less use of it after the crash (Greenspan, 1988, p. 11).
- Another lesson was structural: exchanges needed **more execution capacity**, and the market system needed stronger **clearing, settlement, and capital support** to reduce systemic risk during a crisis (Greenspan, 1988, pp. 11–12, 14).
- Greenspan also stresses a regulatory lesson: because cash, futures, and options markets are tightly connected, regulators need **better coordination across markets and across countries** instead of treating each market in isolation (Greenspan, 1988, p. 13).

## Source Citation

Greenspan, A. (1988, December 28). *Remarks by Alan Greenspan, Chairman, Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, before a Joint Meeting of the American Economic Association and the American Finance Association, New York, New York*.
