# Source note for *Portfolio Insurance and Other Investor Fashions as Factors in the 1987 Stock Market Crash*

- Portfolio insurance was a strategy meant to let big investors stay in a rising stock market while also limiting losses if the market dropped (Shiller, 1988, p. 287).
- In practice, it was designed to create something like a protective put option on a stock portfolio without necessarily buying an actual option contract (Shiller, 1988, p. 289).
- The strategy relied on **dynamic hedging**, which means changing the mix of risky assets and safe assets as prices move rather than setting one fixed hedge and leaving it alone (Shiller, 1988, p. 289).
- The basic logic of dynamic hedging was simple: if prices fall, reduce stock market exposure; if prices rise, increase exposure again (Shiller, 1988, p. 289).
- Portfolio insurers often did this adjustment through **stock index futures** instead of selling all the underlying stocks directly, because futures trading could change exposure quickly and cheaply (Shiller, 1988, pp. 287-288).
- The strategy sells when markets fall because the model says the investor must cut risk to keep the portfolio above its target loss floor (Shiller, 1988, p. 289).
- So a falling market does not tell the strategy to wait. It tells the strategy to sell more as prices move down in order to stay hedged (Shiller, 1988, pp. 288-289).
- Shiller describes this as part of a **cascade effect**: an initial drop leads portfolio insurers to sell, that selling pushes prices down further, and the new decline triggers even more selling (Shiller, 1988, p. 288).
- The danger becomes much greater when many institutions follow the same strategy at the same time, because they all react in the same direction to the same price signal (Shiller, 1988, p. 288).
- Once many large investors start selling together, other traders may also sell in anticipation of further declines, which adds another layer of downward pressure (Shiller, 1988, p. 288).
- This creates a feedback loop: falling prices trigger mechanical selling, that selling deepens the fall, and the deeper fall triggers still more mechanical selling (Shiller, 1988, p. 288).
- Shiller notes that major official studies treated portfolio insurance as an important factor in **accelerating and worsening** the 1987 decline, even if it was not the only cause (Shiller, 1988, p. 288).
- At the same time, he argues the simple “portfolio insurance caused the crash” story is incomplete. He thinks investor psychology and a broader fear of a crash also mattered (Shiller, 1988, pp. 293-294).
- One lesson analysts took from the crash was that trading strategies that look sensible for one institution can become destabilizing when many firms use them together (Shiller, 1988, p. 288).
- Another lesson was that market analysis should not focus only on models and mechanics; it also has to consider investor beliefs, imitation, and social contagion in markets (Shiller, 1988, pp. 293-294).
- Shiller’s broader takeaway is that regulators and researchers need to study both market structure and investor behavior if they want to understand or reduce crash risk (Shiller, 1988, p. 294).

**Source citation**

Shiller, R. J. (1988). *Portfolio insurance and other investor fashions as factors in the 1987 stock market crash*. In S. Fischer (Ed.), *NBER Macroeconomics Annual 1988, Volume 3* (pp. 287-297). MIT Press.
